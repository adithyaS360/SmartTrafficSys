What you are looking at

A traffic signal has to decide how long to hold each direction green. The three junctions above face identical traffic and differ only in how they answer that.

Badly timed gives every direction the same 30 seconds regardless of demand — common wherever timings were set years ago and never revisited. Properly timed is still fixed, but computed from the actual measured flows using Webster's method, the standard since 1958. Adaptive extends green while vehicles are still queued and ends it early once the road clears.

All three receive exactly the same vehicles at exactly the same moments. If each got its own random traffic, the difference between them could just as easily be luck. Feeding all three one arrival stream means every second of divergence comes from the decision-making and nothing else.

The result is smaller than it first looks, and that is the interesting part. Measured against the badly timed junction, adaptive control looks about 38% better at every demand level. But most of that margin belongs to the bad timing, not to adaptive control: simply recomputing the fixed plan from the measured flows captures the bulk of it, for the price of a traffic survey and no new hardware at all.

Against a properly timed plan, adaptive adds roughly 2% at light demand and 24% at rush hour. That pattern makes sense — a well-proportioned fixed plan already matches the average demand ratio, so adaptive only earns its keep where the variance matters. Try Light and then Rush hour and watch the gap open.

The practical reading, if you have a junction in mind: retime it first. It is cheap, it needs no equipment, and it recovers most of what is available. Adaptive control buys you the peak.

The adaptive controller cannot do whatever it likes. Green is never shorter than 10 seconds — drivers who have started moving need time to clear — and never longer than 50, so one busy road cannot starve the other. Those limits live in the signal's state machine rather than the algorithm, so a bad decision can waste time but cannot create an unsafe crossing.

This is a simulation, not live traffic. No camera is attached to it. The detection side of the project — YOLOv8 finding vehicles, tracking them between frames, counting them across a stop line — was validated separately against real footage, but control cannot be measured on a recording: the cars in a video do what they did regardless of what the signal does. Measuring a controller needs a world that reacts, which is what you are watching.
